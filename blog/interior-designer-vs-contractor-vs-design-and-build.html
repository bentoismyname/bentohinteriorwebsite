Interior designer, contractor, or design and build: which one do you need?

By Benjamin Toh·22 Oct 2026·3 min read

Key points

  • A contractor builds what you specify. An interior designer plans, draws and manages. A design-and-build firm does both under one contract.
  • The right choice depends on how much design and coordination you want to carry yourself.
  • For an HDB flat, whoever does the work must be in HDB’s Directory of Renovation Contractors.
  • Ask who actually builds your home: the firm’s own crew, or subcontractors.

The first decision in a renovation is not the style. It is who you hire. I run a design-and-build firm, so I have a side in this, and I will say so plainly further down. First, here are the three options set out as fairly as I can.

The three options, in plain terms

Contractor

A contractor carries out the work. You bring the layout, choose the materials and make the decisions as they come up. This suits straightforward jobs such as repainting, new flooring or a simple kitchen, especially when you know exactly what you want. The trade-off is that the design thinking and the coordination are yours.

Interior designer

A designer starts with how you live, then produces the space plan, drawings, 3D visuals and material selections. Most also coordinate the trades and manage the site. Some design only, and work with a separate builder.

Design and build

One firm designs the home and builds it, under one contract. One party is accountable from the first drawing to the handover, and the drawings are made by people who know how their own crew will build them.

How to choose

  • Scope. If you are hacking walls, moving wet areas or building carpentry across the whole home, design input pays for itself. If you are painting and changing the floor, a good contractor is enough.
  • Time. Site decisions happen on weekdays. Ask yourself honestly whether you can be reached, and whether you can be there.
  • Confidence with paperwork. If you can read a quotation line by line and follow a drawing, you can manage more yourself.
  • Number of trades. The more trades that have to follow one another, the more a single coordinator is worth.

Questions that matter more than the label

  • Who draws, and who builds? Are the carpenters employed by the firm, and where is the carpentry made?
  • Who is on site daily, and how often will the designer visit?
  • For an HDB flat, is the firm listed in HDB’s Directory of Renovation Contractors? HDB requires flat owners to engage only listed contractors. Window works must also be done by a BCA-approved window contractor.
  • How are changes priced and approved once work has started?
  • Can I visit a finished home, or speak to a past client, instead of looking only at renders?
Judge the people and the paperwork, not the label.

Where I stand

I believe design should know how it is built. Drawings made far from the workshop cost clients money. That is why M2D Interior Design, the firm I founded, keeps its design team, its site crew and its carpentry workshop under one roof.

That is my bias, and you should weigh it as one. A good contractor with a clear brief can deliver a very good home. A poor design-and-build firm can still let you down. Whichever route you take, the checks are the same: read the quotation properly, confirm who is building, and get every promise in writing.
